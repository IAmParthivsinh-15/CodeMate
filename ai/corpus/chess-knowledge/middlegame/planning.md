# Planning in the Middlegame

## Definition
Planning in the middlegame means choosing a goal for the next several moves and making moves that work towards it, instead of reacting move by move. A plan grows out of the position: the pawn structure, which side has more space, which pieces are good or bad, and where the weaknesses are. A good plan might be "attack the king on the kingside", "put a knight on the d5 outpost", "win the weak pawn on c6" or "open the c-file and invade on the seventh rank". Even a simple plan is better than moving pieces randomly, which is a common reason players feel lost after the opening.

## How to make a plan
To make a plan in the middlegame, work through a short checklist:
- Safety first: are there any threats against me, and any tactics for me?
- Pawn structure: where are the weak pawns and weak squares for both sides, and which pawn breaks are available?
- King safety: whose king is weaker, and can I attack it?
- Piece activity: which of my pieces is worst, and where should it go?
- Open files and diagonals: which lines are open and who controls them?
Then choose a target (a weakness, a square or the king) and figure out which pieces and pawn moves get you there.

## Typical middlegame plans
Common plans follow from the structure:
- With a space advantage and a closed center, attack on the side where your pawns point.
- With an open center and better development, open lines and attack the king before the opponent consolidates.
- Against weak pawns, fix them so they cannot advance, then attack them with pieces.
- With a queenside pawn majority, advance it to create a passed pawn, often heading for the endgame.
- With a strong outpost, bring a knight there and use it to support an attack.

## Common planning mistakes
Players often go wrong with planning in the middlegame in these ways:
- Having no plan and making aimless moves, such as shuffling a rook back and forth.
- Choosing a plan that ignores the opponent's threats or counterplay.
- Sticking to a plan after the position has changed, for example after a pawn exchange opens a new file.
- Playing on the wrong side of the board, for example attacking on the queenside while the opponent breaks through in the center.
- Making pawn moves just because they look active, creating weaknesses without a clear purpose.

## How to spot it in your games
When you review a game where you felt lost after the opening, pick the position where you first did not know what to do. Go through the checklist: structure, kings, weakest piece, open lines. Write down one plan in a single sentence, such as "bring the knight to e5 and attack f7". Next time you reach a similar position, use the same checklist before moving. When your opponent's plan works easily, ask which move of yours could have stopped it; this links planning to prophylaxis.
