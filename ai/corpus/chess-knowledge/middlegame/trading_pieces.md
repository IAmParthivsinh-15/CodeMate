# Trading Pieces

## Definition
Trading pieces (exchanging) means giving up one of your pieces for an enemy piece of about the same value, such as a knight for a bishop or a rook for a rook. Although the material stays equal, every trade changes the character of the position: it may simplify it, remove an important attacker or defender, change the pawn structure, or lead towards an endgame. Knowing when to trade and when to avoid trades is a key middlegame skill. A good trade removes one of your opponent's best pieces or one of your worst ones.

## When you should trade
Trading pieces usually helps you in these situations:
- You are ahead in material. Trading pieces (not pawns) makes your extra material more decisive; a rule of thumb is "when ahead, trade pieces".
- You are under attack. Exchanging the opponent's attacking pieces reduces the danger to your king.
- Your opponent has a strong piece, such as a knight on an outpost or an active bishop, and you can exchange it for a worse piece of yours.
- You have a better endgame, for example a healthier pawn structure or a more active king.
- You have less space. Trades relieve cramped positions by giving the remaining pieces more room.

## When you should avoid trading
Avoid trading pieces when:
- You are behind in material, because trades bring the opponent closer to a winning endgame.
- You are attacking, since each trade removes attackers and helps the defender.
- You have more space; the side with a cramped position wants trades, so keep pieces on to exploit your space.
- The trade would give up an important piece such as the bishop pair, or your only defender of a weak colour complex.
- The trade would improve your opponent's pawn structure or open a file for their rooks.

## How to decide on a trade
Before trading pieces, ask three questions. First, which piece is better: the one I give up or the one I get? Second, what does the position look like after the trade, including the pawn structure and the recapture? Third, which side benefits from simplification? Also consider the recapture carefully: capturing with a pawn might double your pawns or open a file, while capturing with a piece keeps structure intact. Sometimes giving up more material, like an exchange sacrifice of a rook for a minor piece, is right because it removes a key enemy piece or wrecks their pawns.

## How to spot it in your games
When you review a game, look at every exchange and ask who benefited. Common mistakes include trading your good bishop for a bad knight, trading pieces when you are behind in material, and trading attackers when your attack was strong. If your position became worse after a series of trades, the problem may be that you traded your active pieces while your opponent kept theirs. Next time, before each exchange, compare the pieces and the resulting endgame.
