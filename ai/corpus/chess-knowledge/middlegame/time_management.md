# Time Management

## Definition
Time management is how you use your clock during a game. In every timed game, running out of time loses (unless the opponent cannot possibly checkmate), so the clock is part of the fight. Good time management means spending time where it matters most, such as critical positions, sharp tactics and key decisions, and moving faster in simple or familiar positions. Poor time management leads to time trouble (also called time pressure), when a player has very little time left and starts making hasty, error-prone moves. Many games are lost on the clock or through blunders caused by the clock rather than by a lack of chess skill.

## How to budget your time
A practical approach to time management is to split your time into stages:
- Opening: play known moves quickly, but think when you leave your preparation or when the opponent surprises you.
- Middlegame: spend most of your time here, especially at critical moments, such as before pawn breaks, sacrifices, exchanges into an endgame, or when the position changes character.
- Endgame: keep enough time to calculate precisely; endgames often require exact moves.
Aim to reach the late middlegame with a reasonable reserve. Using increment (extra time added each move) wisely helps: in games with increment you can always play a few safe moves to rebuild time.

## Recognising critical moments
A key time management skill is noticing when a position is critical and deserves extra thought. Warning signs include: many captures or checks are possible, the pawn structure is about to change, kings are exposed, an exchange would lead into an endgame, or your opponent just made an unexpected move. In these moments it is worth spending several minutes. In quiet positions where several moves are similar, choose a reasonable one and save time.

## Common time management mistakes
Players often lose control of the clock in predictable ways:
- Spending a long time in the opening on moves they could play from general principles.
- Trying to calculate everything to the end in a position that does not require it.
- Checking the same line again and again due to indecision.
- Relaxing when ahead and then being forced to play fast in the endgame.
- Playing too quickly when the opponent is short of time, which lets them back into the game.
In time trouble, prioritise safety: make moves that keep pieces protected and the king safe, and use simple checks for blunders.

## How to spot it in your games
When you review a game, write down how much time you spent on each phase and where your mistakes happened. If your blunders cluster when you had little time left, you are spending too long earlier. If you lost quickly in a sharp position you played fast, you did not recognise a critical moment. Next time, set a simple target, such as keeping at least a third of your time after move 20, and use your opponent's thinking time to look at general ideas and threats.
