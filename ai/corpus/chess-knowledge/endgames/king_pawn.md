# King and Pawn Endgames

## Definition
King and pawn endgames are positions where only kings and pawns remain. They are the foundation of all endgame knowledge, because many other endings turn into them after exchanges. The most basic case is king and one pawn against a lone king: the stronger side tries to promote, while the defender tries to block the pawn or reach a stalemate position. Three ideas decide most of these endings: the opposition, key squares, and the rule of the square. Mastering them lets you decide quickly whether a pawn ending is winning or drawn before you enter it.

## The opposition
The opposition is a standoff between kings on the same file (or rank) with exactly one square between them. The side that is not to move "has the opposition", because the other king must give way. Example: White king e5, White pawn e4, Black king e7. If Black is to move, Black must step aside: after `1...Kd7 2.Kf6` or `1...Kf7 2.Kd6`, White's king advances and the pawn promotes. If White is to move, Black keeps the opposition by answering each king move with the mirror move, and the game is drawn. When the defending king can take the opposition in front of the pawn, the defender usually draws.

## Key squares
Key squares are squares that, if the attacking king reaches one of them, guarantee promotion no matter who is to move. For a knight, bishop or center pawn on its second, third or fourth rank, the key squares are the three squares two ranks in front of it: for a pawn on e4 they are d6, e6 and f6. Once the pawn reaches the fifth or sixth rank, the squares one rank in front also become key squares (for a pawn on e5: d6, e6, f6, d7, e7 and f7). For example, with White king e6, pawn e5 and Black king e8, White wins whoever moves: `1.Kd6 Kd8 2.e6 Ke8 3.e7 Kf7 4.Kd7` and the pawn promotes. Rook pawns are special: the defender draws by reaching the corner in front of the pawn.

## The rule of the square
The rule of the square tells you whether a king can catch a passed pawn with no help from other pieces. Imagine a square whose one side runs from the pawn to its promotion square. If the defending king can step inside this square on its move, it catches the pawn; if not, the pawn promotes. For example, a White pawn on a4 creates a square with corners a4, a8, e8 and e4. A Black king on f5, with Black to move, plays `...Ke5` (or `...Ke4` or `...Ke6`) and enters the square in time; with White to move, `a5` shrinks the square and the king on f5 is too late. Remember that a pawn still on its starting square can move two squares, so count it as if it were already one rank further.

## Common mistakes
Typical errors in king and pawn endgames include:
- Pushing the pawn too early instead of advancing the king in front of it.
- Losing the opposition by moving the king to the wrong square, for example walking straight at the enemy king when a side step keeps the opposition.
- With king and pawn against king, pushing the pawn to the seventh rank when it only produces stalemate (White king e6, pawn e7 and Black king e8 with Black to move is stalemate).
- Trading into a pawn ending without counting: pawn endings are usually decided by precise calculation.

## What to look for next time
Before entering a king and pawn endgame, check three things: can my king reach a key square, who will have the opposition, and can the enemy king catch my passed pawn using the rule of the square? Lead with the king, not the pawn. When defending, keep your king in front of the pawn and take the opposition whenever possible; with a rook pawn, run to the corner.
