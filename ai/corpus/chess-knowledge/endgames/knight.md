# Knight Endgames

## Definition
Knight endgames are positions where each side has a knight (or a knight faces pawns or a bishop) plus pawns. The knight is a short-range piece: it needs several moves to cross the board, it cannot "lose a move" (it always changes square colour when it moves, so it cannot hold its position and pass the turn), and it controls squares of only one colour at a time. Because of this, knight endgames behave a lot like king and pawn endgames: the side with the more active king and the better pawn structure usually wins, and a far-advanced or outside passed pawn is especially strong.

## Knight endings are like pawn endings
The saying "knight endings are like pawn endings", associated with Mikhail Botvinnik, means that the same factors decide both. Extra pawns usually convert well because the knight cannot defend from a distance. An outside passed pawn is powerful, because the defending knight struggles to cover both wings. King activity is crucial: bring the king to the center or to the pawns. Tactics based on knight forks and zugzwang are frequent. Many knight endgames can be calculated as if they were pawn endings, with extra care for forks.

## Knight against pawns
A knight alone against a passed pawn can usually stop it if the knight can reach a square in front of the pawn or control the promotion square in time. The knight has the most trouble against rook pawns (a- and h-pawns), because near the corner it has fewer squares, and against a pawn supported by its king. Two connected passed pawns far advanced can often beat a knight. Remember also that a king and a single knight cannot checkmate a lone king, and even two knights cannot force checkmate against a lone king, so trading into such positions with no pawns left is a draw.

## Knight against bishop
In knight versus bishop endgames, the bishop is usually better in open positions with pawns on both wings, because it can cover distant squares quickly. The knight is better when all pawns are on one side of the board, when the position is closed, or when the enemy pawns are fixed on squares of one colour that the knight can attack while the bishop cannot defend them. A knight with a secure central outpost can dominate a bad bishop.

## What to look for next time
In your knight endgames, count tempi as carefully as you would in a pawn ending: how many moves does the knight need to reach a key square? Activate your king, create an outside passed pawn if you can, and watch for knight forks against king and pawns. When defending with a knight, keep it close to the enemy passed pawn and in front of it. A common mistake is placing the knight on the edge, far from the action, where it needs too many moves to get back.
