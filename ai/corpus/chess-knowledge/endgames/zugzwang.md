# Zugzwang

## Definition
Zugzwang (German for "compulsion to move") is a situation where the player to move would prefer to pass, because every legal move makes their position worse. Chess does not allow passing, so the player in zugzwang must damage their own position. Zugzwang is most common in endgames, especially king and pawn endgames, where there are few pieces and few spare moves. It can also occur in the middlegame when one side's pieces are completely tied up. A position where whoever is to move is in zugzwang is called mutual zugzwang.

## Example of zugzwang
The opposition in king and pawn endgames is a form of zugzwang. With White king e5, White pawn e4 and Black king e7, if Black is to move Black must step aside and let the White king advance to d6 or f6, after which the pawn promotes. A clear example of mutual zugzwang is the trebuchet: White king c6, White pawn d5, Black king e5, Black pawn d6. Each king defends its own pawn and attacks the other. Whoever must move has to abandon their pawn: if White moves, Black plays `...Kxd5`; if Black moves, White plays `Kxd6`. The side that is not to move wins the pawn and usually the game.

## Using spare tempi and triangulation
To win a zugzwang position, you often need to pass the move to your opponent. There are two main ways:
- Spare pawn moves (tempo moves): a pawn move that does not change the position much, used to transfer the obligation to move. Keeping pawns unmoved in an endgame can preserve these tempi.
- Triangulation: a king manoeuvre around a small triangle of three squares, returning to the original square in three moves. If the opponent's king has too few safe squares to copy this, the same position arises with the other side to move.
Knights cannot lose a tempo by themselves, but kings, bishops, rooks and queens often can.

## How to spot it in your games
Zugzwang is hard to see unless you look for it. In endgames, ask: what would happen if my opponent had to move here? If their every move worsens their position, look for a way to reach the same position with them to move, using a spare pawn move or triangulation. When defending, be careful not to use up your pawn moves early, and look for positions where you have spare tempi. A common mistake is pushing pawns carelessly in a king and pawn ending, which throws away tempo moves you might need later.

## Key takeaways
Zugzwang means every move worsens your position, and it is decisive in many endgames. Opposition and the trebuchet are basic examples. Winning usually requires passing the move, using spare pawn moves or triangulation. In pawn endings, count spare tempi for both sides before committing. In the middlegame zugzwang is rarer, but it can decide games when one side's pieces are so tied up that any move drops material or lets the opponent break through.
