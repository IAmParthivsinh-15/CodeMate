# Rook Endgames

## Definition
Rook endgames are positions where each side has a rook (sometimes more) plus pawns. They are the most common type of endgame in practice, so knowing a few key positions pays off in many games. Two positions with a rook and pawn against a rook form the backbone of the theory: the Lucena position, which is a win for the side with the pawn, and the Philidor position, which is a draw for the defender. Rook endgames are famous for being drawish: even a pawn up, the stronger side often cannot win if the defender knows the right technique.

## The Lucena position: building a bridge
The Lucena position is the key winning position in rook endgames. The attacker's king stands on the promotion square in front of its pawn on the seventh rank, and the defending king is cut off along a file. Example: White king b8, pawn b7, rook c1; Black king d7, rook a2. White wins by building a bridge: `1.Rd1+ Ke7 2.Rd4!` The rook goes to the fourth rank to shield the king from checks later. After a waiting move such as `2...Ra1`, White plays `3.Kc7 Rc1+ 4.Kb6 Rb1+ 5.Kc6 Rc1+ 6.Kb5 Rb1+ 7.Rb4`, the rook blocks the checks and the pawn promotes.

## The Philidor position: third-rank defence
The Philidor position shows how the defender draws a rook and pawn against rook ending when the defending king is in front of the pawn. The defender keeps the rook on their third rank (the sixth rank when Black defends), preventing the attacking king from advancing to that rank. Example: Black king e8, Black rook on the sixth rank, White pawn on e5 and White king on d5 or f5. As long as the pawn stays on e5, the rook waits on the sixth rank. When White pushes the pawn to e6, the pawn no longer shelters White's king, and Black's rook goes to the first rank and gives checks from behind, which the king cannot escape. The result is a draw.

## Rook behind the passed pawn
A central principle of rook endgames, often linked to Siegbert Tarrasch, is that rooks belong behind passed pawns, both your own and your opponent's. A rook behind your own passed pawn supports it and gains more squares as the pawn advances. A rook behind the enemy passed pawn attacks it from behind and keeps its activity as the pawn moves forward. In contrast, a rook placed in front of a passed pawn becomes passive, because it must stay there to blockade. Rook activity in general is vital: an active rook is often worth a pawn.

## What to look for next time
In your rook endgames, remember: put rooks behind passed pawns, keep your rook active even at the cost of a pawn, and cut off the enemy king along a rank or file. When you have a rook and pawn against a rook, aim for the Lucena position and build a bridge on the fourth rank. When defending, get your king in front of the pawn and use the Philidor third-rank defence, switching to checks from behind once the pawn advances. A common mistake is letting your rook become passive, tied to defending a pawn from the side or front.
