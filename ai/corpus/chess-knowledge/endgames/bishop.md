# Bishop Endgames

## Good bishop and bad bishop
In bishop endgames, the quality of a bishop depends mostly on its own pawns. A bad bishop is one whose own pawns stand on squares of the same colour as the bishop, so they block its diagonals and it has little to attack. A good bishop is one whose pawns stand on the opposite colour, so its diagonals are open and it can attack enemy pawns on its own colour. A bishop endgame of good bishop against bad bishop can often be won even with equal pawns, because the bad bishop cannot defend its pawns well and the stronger side's king can infiltrate on the squares of the other colour.

## The wrong-coloured rook pawn
The wrong-coloured bishop is a famous exception in bishop endgames. With a king, bishop and rook pawn (a- or h-pawn) against a lone king, if the bishop does not control the promotion square, the defender draws by getting the king into that corner. For example, a White h-pawn promotes on h8, which is a dark square; if White has a light-squared bishop and the Black king reaches h8 (or the squares around it), White cannot drive it out and any attempt leads to stalemate or a draw. Similarly, an a-pawn promotes on a8, a light square, so a dark-squared bishop is the wrong bishop for it.

## Same-coloured and opposite-coloured bishops
Bishop endgames split into two types. With bishops of the same colour, both bishops fight for the same squares; the side with the better bishop, more active king and better pawn structure usually decides the game. With opposite-coloured bishops (one light-squared, one dark-squared), the endgame is very drawish, because the defending bishop can blockade passed pawns on its colour and the attacking bishop can never challenge it. Even two extra pawns are sometimes not enough to win. In the middlegame, however, opposite-coloured bishops favour the attacker, since each bishop attacks squares the other cannot defend.

## Technique in bishop endgames
Key techniques in bishop endgames include:
- Place your pawns on the opposite colour from your bishop, so they do not block it and are not attacked by the enemy bishop if it is of the same colour.
- Use your king actively, especially to invade on the colour your opponent's bishop cannot control.
- Create passed pawns on both sides of the board; bishops are long-range pieces, but they struggle to stop two passed pawns far apart.
- With opposite-coloured bishops, try to create a passed pawn on each wing, as a single passed pawn is easily blockaded.

## What to look for next time
Before entering a bishop endgame, check which colour your pawns and your opponent's pawns stand on, and whether the bishops are the same or opposite colour. If you are winning with a rook pawn and bishop, check that your bishop controls the promotion square; if not, keep the enemy king out of the corner. If you are defending a worse position, aim for opposite-coloured bishops or the wrong-coloured bishop draw. A common mistake is putting all your pawns on your bishop's colour, turning it into a bad bishop.
