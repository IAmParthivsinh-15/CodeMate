# Opening Principles

## Definition
Opening principles are the general guidelines for the first ten to fifteen moves of a game. They help you play good moves even when you do not know any opening theory. The goals of the opening are to control the center, develop your pieces to active squares, bring your king to safety, and connect your rooks. A player who follows opening principles usually reaches a playable middlegame, while a player who ignores them often falls behind in development and gets attacked early.

## The core opening principles
The most important opening principles for the first ten moves are:
- Control the center with pawns, typically `1.e4` or `1.d4` (or `...e5` / `...d5` for Black), and with pieces aimed at the central squares.
- Develop knights and bishops quickly, usually knights before bishops, to squares like f3, c3, c4, b5, f4 or g5.
- Castle early, usually kingside, to protect the king and bring a rook towards the center.
- Move each piece once in the opening unless there is a good reason, such as a capture or a threat.
- Do not bring the queen out too early, where it can be chased with tempo by minor pieces.
- Connect your rooks by developing all minor pieces and the queen off the back rank, then place rooks on open or central files.

## What to do in the first 10 moves
A practical plan for your first 10 moves using opening principles: play one central pawn move, develop both knights, develop the bishops, castle, and then make a second central pawn move or bring the queen to a modest square such as e2, d2 or c2. After `1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.O-O Nf6 5.d3 d6 6.c3`, for example, both sides have followed the principles, and each can think about middlegame plans. Before each move, also check your opponent's threats; principles never replace a blunder check.

## Common opening mistakes
Typical mistakes that break opening principles:
- Moving many pawns, especially edge pawns like `a4` or `h4`, instead of developing pieces.
- Early queen adventures that allow the opponent to develop with gain of time by attacking the queen.
- Leaving the king in the center while the center is opening.
- Grabbing pawns at the cost of development, particularly the b2 or b7 pawn with the queen.
- Moving the f-pawn early (`f3` or `...f6`), which weakens the king's diagonal and was the cause of Fool's mate, `1.f3 e5 2.g4 Qh4#`.
- Falling for early checkmate ideas against f7 or f2, such as Scholar's mate.

## How to spot it in your games
After each game, look at your first ten moves and count: how many pieces did I develop, did I castle, and how many pawn moves or queen moves did I make? If your opponent had more pieces developed and a safer king by move ten, you probably broke an opening principle. Also note any move where your opponent gained time by attacking one of your pieces, often the queen, and ask where that piece could have gone instead.
