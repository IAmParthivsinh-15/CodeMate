# Piece Values

## Definition
Piece values are a simple point system that helps you judge whether a trade is good for you. The traditional values are:
- Pawn: 1 point
- Knight: 3 points
- Bishop: 3 points (often considered slightly more than a knight)
- Rook: 5 points
- Queen: 9 points
The king has no point value because losing it means losing the game. Adding up the points each side has gives the material balance. If you give up a knight (3) and win a rook (5), you have gained two points. These numbers are guidelines rather than laws, but in most positions the side with more material is better.

## Common trades and their names
Piece values give names to several common exchanges. Trading a bishop for a knight is an even trade. Winning a rook for a bishop or knight is called "winning the exchange", worth about two points. A rook plus a pawn is roughly equal to two minor pieces, although two minor pieces are often stronger in the middlegame. Two rooks (10) are roughly equal to a queen (9). Three minor pieces (9) are roughly equal to a queen too. Keeping both bishops, the bishop pair, is usually worth an extra half pawn or so, especially in open positions.

## When piece values mislead
Piece values are averages, and the real worth of a piece depends on the position. A knight on a strong central outpost can be better than a rook with no open file. A bishop blocked by its own pawns may be worth less than a knight. Passed pawns grow in value as they advance: a pawn on the seventh rank can be worth as much as a piece. Material also matters less than king safety: giving up a piece to deliver checkmate is always good. Finally, activity and time count. In the opening, grabbing a pawn with your queen while falling far behind in development can lose quickly.

## How to spot it in your games
Before every capture or trade, count the piece values that will change hands. Ask: what do I give, what do I get, and what will the position look like afterwards? Common mistakes to watch for:
- Giving a rook for a knight or bishop without getting anything back.
- Trading your active pieces for your opponent's passive ones.
- Grabbing a pawn and allowing a much stronger attack in return.
- Forgetting that a piece that looks protected can be attacked twice and won.
When you are ahead in material, trading pieces (not pawns) usually makes your extra material count for more. When you are behind, avoid trades and look for activity and counterplay.
