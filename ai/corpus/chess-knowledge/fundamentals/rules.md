# Rules of Chess

## Board and setup
The rules of chess start with the board: 64 squares in an 8x8 grid, set up so that each player has a light square in the right-hand corner (h1 for White, a8 for Black). Each side begins with 16 pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns. The rooks go in the corners, then the knights, then the bishops. The queen stands on the square of her own colour (White queen on d1, Black queen on d8) and the king goes next to her on the e-file. Pawns fill the second rank for White and the seventh rank for Black. White always moves first, and the players then alternate, one move each turn. Passing is not allowed: if it is your turn, you must make a legal move.

## How the pieces move
Under the rules of chess every piece has its own movement pattern:
- King: one square in any direction. It may never move onto a square attacked by an enemy piece.
- Queen: any number of squares along ranks, files and diagonals.
- Rook: any number of squares along ranks and files.
- Bishop: any number of squares diagonally, so it stays on one colour all game.
- Knight: an L-shape (two squares one way, then one square at a right angle). It is the only piece that jumps over others.
- Pawn: one square straight forward, or two squares from its starting square; it captures one square diagonally forward.

No piece except the knight can jump, and you capture by moving onto a square occupied by an enemy piece, which is removed. Castling, en passant and promotion are special moves covered separately.

## Check, checkmate and stalemate
Check is the central rule of chess: a king is in check when an enemy piece attacks it. A player in check must get out of it immediately by moving the king, capturing the checking piece, or blocking the line of attack (a knight check cannot be blocked). You may never make a move that leaves your own king in check. Checkmate happens when the king is in check and there is no legal way out; the game ends at once and the side that delivered mate wins. Stalemate is different: the player to move is not in check but has no legal move at all. Stalemate is a draw, not a win, which is why a player far ahead in material must be careful not to take away every move from the opponent's king.

## How a game can be drawn
The rules of chess allow several kinds of draw besides stalemate:
- Agreement: both players agree to a draw.
- Threefold repetition: the same position occurs three times with the same player to move and the same castling and en passant rights. A player can claim it (and under FIDE rules a fivefold repetition ends the game automatically).
- Fifty-move rule: fifty moves by each side pass with no capture and no pawn move; a draw can be claimed (seventy-five moves ends the game automatically under FIDE rules).
- Insufficient material: neither side can possibly checkmate, for example king versus king, or king and a single bishop or knight versus king.
If a player runs out of time, they lose, unless the opponent has no possible way to deliver checkmate, in which case the game is drawn.

## What to look for next time
When a rule of chess surprises you in a game, check these points before moving:
- Is my king in check? If so, list every way out: move, capture, or block.
- Does my intended move leave my king exposed to an attack, for example by moving a piece that stands between my king and an enemy rook or bishop? That move is illegal.
- When I am winning, does my opponent still have a legal move after my move? If not, and they are not in check, I have stalemated them and thrown away the win.
- In over-the-board play, the touch-move rule applies: if you deliberately touch one of your pieces you must move it, and if you touch an opponent's piece you must capture it, if legally possible.
