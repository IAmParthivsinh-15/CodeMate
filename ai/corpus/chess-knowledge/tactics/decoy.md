# Decoy

## Definition
A decoy (also called attraction) is a tactic that lures an enemy piece, very often the king, onto a particular square where it becomes the target of another tactic. Usually a sacrifice is offered on that square: when the enemy piece captures, it lands exactly where you want it. The follow-up is typically a fork, a skewer, a pin or a checkmate. A decoy differs from a deflection: deflection pulls a piece away from a square it was guarding, while a decoy pulls a piece to a square where it will be hit.

## Example of a decoy
Here is a decoy that sets up a knight fork. White: king g1, rook h1, knight g5, pawn f6. Black: king g8, queen d6. White plays `1.Rh8+!`. The king cannot go to f7 (covered by the knight), g7 (covered by the pawn) or h7 (covered by rook and knight), and f8 is on the rook's rank, so the only legal move is `1...Kxh8`. The king has been decoyed to h8. Now `2.Nf7+` forks the king on h8 and the queen on d6, and after the king moves, `3.Nxd6` wins the queen. White gave a rook to win a queen.

## Common decoy patterns
To find decoys, learn the typical ones:
- A rook or queen sacrifice on the back rank or in the corner that draws the king onto a square where a knight can fork it with the queen.
- A bishop sacrifice on f7 (or f2), such as `Bxf7+ Kxf7`, drawing the king forward so that a knight check like `Ng5+` or a queen check can follow.
- A sacrifice that draws the enemy queen onto a square where it can be skewered or trapped.
- A sacrifice that pulls a king in front of its own pieces so that a discovered check or a mating net follows.

## How to spot it in your games
To spot a decoy, look for squares where a fork, skewer or mate would work if only the enemy king or queen were standing there. Then ask whether you can force that piece onto the square with a check or a capture it cannot refuse. Defensively, when your opponent offers a piece, especially with check, ask where your capturing piece will end up and what enemy pieces attack or can reach that square next move. Many games are lost because a player accepts a sacrifice automatically without checking what happens on the square where their king or queen lands.

## Key takeaways
A decoy forces an enemy piece onto a bad square, usually with a sacrifice that must be accepted. It is almost always a two-step idea: attraction first, then a fork, skewer, pin or mate. Forcing moves (checks and captures) make decoys work because they leave the opponent no choice. Before accepting any sacrifice, visualise the position with your piece on the new square and look for the follow-up.
