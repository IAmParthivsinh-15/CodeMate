# Fork

## Definition
A fork is a tactic in which one piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time. The opponent can usually save only one of them, so the forking side wins material. Any piece can fork, but knights are the most famous forkers because their L-shaped jump attacks squares that other pieces cannot, and a knight cannot be blocked. A fork that includes the king is especially strong because the check must be answered first, leaving the other target undefended. A knight fork of king and queen is sometimes called a royal fork, and a knight attacking king, queen and rook together is often called a family fork.

## How to recognise a fork
To recognise a fork chance, look for enemy pieces that are a knight's jump apart from one common square, or that stand on the same line or diagonal as one of your queen's or bishop's possible destinations. Typical warning signs:
- Enemy king and queen, or king and rook, on squares of the same colour and a knight's move from a free square.
- Undefended pieces, which are ideal targets because any attack on them is a real threat.
- Pieces lined up on the same rank, such as a rook on a8 and a king on e8; a knight on c7 attacks both.
- Two pieces standing side by side in front of your pawn, so that a pawn push attacks both.

## Example: the fork trick
The fork trick is a well-known pawn fork in the opening. After `1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nf6 4.Nc3`, Black can play `4...Nxe4!`, apparently giving up the knight after `5.Nxe4`. But then `5...d5!` is a pawn fork: the pawn on d5 attacks both the bishop on c4 and the knight on e4. White can save only one piece, so Black wins the piece back and gets a good share of the centre. The lesson is that a pawn fork is very powerful, because pawns are the least valuable attacker and any piece they attack is threatened with real loss.

## How to spot it in your games
To avoid falling into a fork, and to find forks for yourself, run a quick check on every move:
- Where can each of my knights jump next move, and what would it attack from there?
- Which of my pieces are a knight's jump away from a square my opponent's knight can reach?
- Are my king and queen on the same colour and close together? That makes royal forks possible.
- Can a pawn push attack two pieces side by side?
A common mistake is to leave pieces undefended, because a fork only needs to hit two loose pieces to win material. If you just lost material to a fork, look at where the forking piece landed and ask whether you could have covered that square or moved one target in advance.

## Key takeaways
A fork wins material by creating two threats with a single move. Knights and pawns are the most common forkers; queens fork often too because they move in every direction. Checks make forks stronger because the opponent has no time to save the second piece. Keep your pieces defended and away from knight-jump patterns, and always look one move ahead at the squares your opponent's knights can reach.
