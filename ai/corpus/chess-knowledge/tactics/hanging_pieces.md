# Hanging Pieces

## Definition
A hanging piece is a piece that can be captured for free, either because it is not defended at all or because it is attacked more times than it is defended. Leaving a piece hanging is the most common way games are lost at club level. A related idea is the loose piece: a piece that is undefended but not yet attacked. Loose pieces are not lost yet, but they are the natural targets of forks, double attacks and discovered attacks. Grandmaster John Nunn summed this up with the saying "Loose Pieces Drop Off", often shortened to LPDO.

## Why loose pieces drop off
LPDO means that undefended pieces tend to get lost, even if nothing attacks them right now. The reason is that almost every tactic needs a target that cannot simply be defended in one move. If two of your pieces are loose, one enemy move that hits both of them, such as a knight fork or a queen move to the middle of the board, wins material. If all your pieces are protected, most double attacks only lead to trades. Keeping pieces protected makes your position resistant to tactics you have not even seen.

## Counting attackers and defenders
To tell whether a piece is truly hanging, count the attackers and defenders on its square:
- If it is attacked and has no defenders, it hangs.
- If attackers outnumber defenders, it can usually be won, as long as the exchanges go in the right order.
- Look at values too: a knight defended once but attacked by a pawn is still in trouble, because pawn takes knight wins material even if you recapture.
- Remember that a pinned defender may not really count, because it cannot legally or safely recapture.
Count again after every capture in the sequence, since the balance can change.

## How to spot it in your games
Before every move, do a quick blunder check:
- Which of my pieces are attacked right now, and are they defended enough?
- After my move, which pieces are left loose, and what could my opponent attack?
- Did my last move stop defending something? A piece that moves often abandons a defensive duty.
Also check the opponent's side: after each of their moves, ask "what does that move attack?" and "did they leave anything hanging?". Many free pieces are missed simply because players do not look for them. If you lost a piece for nothing in your game, find the move where it became undefended and note what question would have caught it.

## Key takeaways
A hanging piece can be taken for free, and a loose piece is one attack away from hanging. The LPDO principle, "Loose Pieces Drop Off", reminds you to keep your pieces protected. Count attackers and defenders, consider the value of the attacking piece, and run a short blunder check before every move. Most beginner and intermediate games are decided by a hanging piece rather than by deep strategy.
