# Pin

## Definition
A pin is a tactic in which a bishop, rook or queen attacks an enemy piece that cannot move, or should not move, because a more valuable piece stands behind it on the same line. In an absolute pin the piece behind is the king, so the pinned piece is not allowed to move off the line at all: doing so would expose the king to check, which is illegal. This is why your knight sometimes cannot move when it stands between your king and an enemy bishop. In a relative pin the piece behind is something valuable but not the king, such as the queen or a rook. Moving the pinned piece is legal, but it would lose the more valuable piece behind it.

## Examples of pins
Two common opening pins show the difference between absolute and relative pins. After `1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4`, the bishop on b4 pins the White knight on c3 against the king on e1 along the diagonal b4-c3-d2-e1. This is an absolute pin: the knight on c3 cannot legally move. After `1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 Bg4`, the bishop on g4 pins the knight on f3 against the queen on d1. This is a relative pin: the knight may move, but then the bishop could capture the queen.

## How to exploit a pin
To exploit a pin, treat the pinned piece as if it were frozen, and then attack it again. Useful ideas:
- Attack the pinned piece with a pawn. It cannot run away, so it may simply be lost.
- Pile up more attackers on the pinned piece than there are defenders.
- Remember that a pinned piece does not really defend anything along other lines; in an absolute pin it cannot recapture off the line.
- Pin a piece to the king and then add a second threat elsewhere.

## How to defend against a pin
When one of your pieces is pinned, you have a few ways out. You can move the valuable piece behind it out of the line (for example, move the queen off the pinning diagonal), put another piece in between (called "interposing" or blocking), attack the pinning piece, or break the pin by driving the pinning piece away with a pawn, often with `h3` or `...h6` against a bishop on g4 or g5. Be careful with relative pins: they can be broken, and a player who relies on them can be punished. In Legal's trap, `1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Bc4 Bg4 4.Nc3 g6? 5.Nxe5! Bxd1?? 6.Bxf7+ Ke7 7.Nd5#`, White ignores the relative pin on f3 and gives up the queen for checkmate.

## How to spot it in your games
To see pins before they hurt you, look along every line from an enemy bishop, rook or queen towards your king and queen. Ask:
- Is my king or queen on the same diagonal, rank or file as an enemy long-range piece, with only one of my pieces in between?
- If I move this piece, what line opens behind it?
- Can my opponent add attackers to my pinned piece faster than I can defend it?
A common mistake is placing the queen and king on the same line as an enemy rook or bishop. Another is assuming a pinned piece still defends a square when it actually cannot move.
