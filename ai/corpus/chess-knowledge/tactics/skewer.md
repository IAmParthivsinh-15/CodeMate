# Skewer

## Definition
A skewer is a tactic in which a bishop, rook or queen attacks a valuable piece that stands in front of a less valuable (or equally valuable) piece on the same line. When the valuable piece moves out of the way, the piece behind it is captured. A skewer is like a pin in reverse: in a pin the less valuable piece is in front, while in a skewer the more valuable piece is in front and is forced to step aside. Skewers against the king are the most powerful, because a king in check must move, and the piece behind it is then lost.

## Example of a skewer
Here is a simple skewer with a bishop. Suppose the Black king stands on d5 and the Black queen on f7, both on the diagonal that runs b3-c4-d5-e6-f7. White plays `Bb3+`. The king is in check and must step off the diagonal, for example `...Ke5`, and then White plays `Bxf7`, winning the queen for a bishop. Rook skewers are also common in endgames: a rook checks a king along a rank or file, the king moves, and the rook captures an enemy rook or a newly promoted queen standing behind the king on the same line.

## How to exploit a skewer
To use a skewer, look for two enemy pieces on the same rank, file or diagonal with nothing between them, where the front one is the more valuable. Checks are ideal because they force the king to move. The skewering piece should be safe on its square, or the trade should still favour you if it is captured. In endgames, watch for the moment an opponent promotes a pawn: if their new queen and king end up on the same line, a rook or bishop check can skewer them and win the queen.

## How to defend against a skewer
To avoid being skewered, do not line up your king with your queen or rooks on an open line that an enemy bishop, rook or queen can reach. If a skewer is coming, you can:
- Move the rear piece first so that it is protected or out of the line.
- Defend the rear piece so that the capture is only a trade.
- Put a piece between the attacker and your valuable piece, if that square is available.
Kings in the endgame are particularly vulnerable because they often walk into lines with their own rooks behind them.

## How to spot it in your games
When you review a game where you lost a piece to a skewer, look for the moment your two pieces first ended up on the same line. Next time, before each move ask: after this move, are my king and queen, or king and rook, standing on one diagonal, rank or file? Can an enemy long-range piece reach that line with check? Scanning for aligned pieces takes a few seconds and prevents most skewers. Offensively, look for enemy pieces lined up the same way; a check that uncovers a capture behind the king usually wins material.
