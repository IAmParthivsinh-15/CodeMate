# Prophylaxis

## Definition
Prophylaxis is the strategy of preventing your opponent's plans before they happen. Instead of only asking "what do I want to do?", you also ask "what does my opponent want to do, and how can I stop it?". A prophylactic move might take away a key square, prevent a pawn break, remove a piece's best route, or make your own position safe against a coming tactic. The idea was developed and popularised by Aron Nimzowitsch and later refined by players such as Tigran Petrosian and Anatoly Karpov, who were famous for smothering their opponents' counterplay.

## Examples of prophylactic moves
Prophylaxis shows up in many ordinary moves:
- Playing `h3` to stop an enemy bishop or knight from coming to g4.
- Playing `a3` (or `...a6`) to stop a piece from using the b4 (or b5) square.
- Making luft with `h3` or `...h6` before starting action elsewhere, so that back-rank tactics are no longer possible.
- Moving your king out of a pin or away from a diagonal that could open later, such as `Kh1` to step off the g1-a7 diagonal.
- Placing a rook on a file where your opponent intends a pawn break, so the break loses its force.

## How to think prophylactically
To use prophylaxis, build a simple habit into your thinking. After your opponent's move, and before choosing yours, ask:
- What is my opponent threatening right now?
- If it were their move again, what would they play?
- What is their long-term plan, and which pawn break or piece move is its key?
Then compare your candidate moves not only by what they achieve for you, but also by how much they disrupt the opponent's plan. Often the best move quietly makes the opponent's best idea impossible.

## Common mistakes
Players struggle with prophylaxis in two opposite ways. Some ignore the opponent completely and follow their own plan, walking into tactics or allowing an unstoppable counterattack. Others become too passive, making only preventive moves and never improving their own position. Good prophylaxis prevents the opponent's most dangerous idea while keeping your own plan moving. Also avoid prophylactic pawn moves that create new weaknesses, such as pushing a pawn in front of your king when it is not needed.

## How to spot it in your games
When you review a game in which your opponent's plan worked smoothly, find the moment the plan became visible, for example when they prepared a pawn break or routed a knight towards an outpost. Ask what single move could have prevented it at that point. Next time, before each move, spend a few seconds asking "what does my opponent want?" This question alone avoids many blunders and turns many games that feel like a struggle into comfortable ones.
