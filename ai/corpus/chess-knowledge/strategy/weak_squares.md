# Weak Squares

## Definition
A weak square is a square that can no longer be defended by one side's pawns. Because pawns only move forward, once the pawns on the neighbouring files have advanced past a square or disappeared, no pawn can ever guard that square again. Such a permanently weak square is called a hole. An enemy piece placed on it, especially a knight, cannot be driven away by a pawn. Weak squares are most important when they are in your own half of the board, near your king, or directly in front of a weak pawn.

## How weak squares are created
Weak squares are usually created by pawn moves. Some examples:
- In the Open Sicilian, Black's c-pawn has already been exchanged on d4. Once Black also plays `...e5` (with the d-pawn on d6), no Black pawn can ever cover d5 again, so it becomes a hole.
- Pushing `g3` (or `...g6`) weakens f3 and h3 (or f6 and h6); if the fianchettoed bishop is then exchanged, those squares become serious holes near the king.
- Advancing pawns on both sides of a square, such as `...c5` and `...e5`, leaves d5 without pawn protection.
Every pawn advance gains space but gives up control of the squares it passed.

## Colour complexes
When several weak squares share one colour, the side suffers from a weak colour complex. This happens when pawns are fixed on one colour and the bishop of the other colour has been exchanged. For example, if Black's pawns stand on e6, d5 and f7 (light squares) and Black has traded the dark-squared bishop, the dark squares such as d6, e5, f6 and h6 may be very weak. Pieces of the opponent, particularly a knight or a dark-squared bishop and the queen, can then invade on those squares.

## How to exploit weak squares
To exploit weak squares:
- Occupy the hole with a piece, ideally a knight, which is hard to dislodge there.
- Exchange the defender of the weak squares, for example the bishop that controls that colour.
- Aim several pieces at the weak squares near the enemy king.
- Keep the pawn structure fixed so the weakness cannot be repaired.

## How to spot it in your games
Before pushing a pawn, ask which squares it stops guarding and whether any of them could become a permanent hole. Pay extra attention to pawn moves in front of your king and to trades of the bishop that matches your weak colour. When your opponent's pieces keep landing on a square in your camp that you cannot chase away with a pawn, identify which pawn move created that weak square, so you can avoid similar moves in future games.
