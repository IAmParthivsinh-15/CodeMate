# Piece Activity

## Definition
Piece activity describes how much each piece is doing: how many squares it controls, whether it attacks targets, and whether it can join the action quickly. An active piece is centralised, has open lines, and works together with the other pieces. A passive piece sits on a square where it controls little, is blocked by its own pawns, or is tied to defending something. Piece activity often matters as much as material: a player a pawn down with much more active pieces can be better, and many endgames are won simply by having the more active king and rook.

## Signs of good and bad pieces
Recognising piece activity starts with looking at each piece in turn:
- Knights are active on central squares and outposts, and poor on the rim ("a knight on the rim is dim").
- Bishops are active on long open diagonals and passive when blocked by their own pawns on the same colour.
- Rooks are active on open files and the seventh rank, and passive behind their own pawns.
- The queen is strong when centralised and safe, but can be chased around if developed too early.
- In the endgame, the king is active in the center and near the pawns.

## Improving your worst piece
A practical way to raise piece activity is to find your worst piece and improve it. When there is no immediate tactic, look for the piece doing the least, then ask where it would be most useful and how to get it there, even if it takes several moves. For example, a knight on b1 might travel to d2, f1 and g3, or a rook might move from a1 to an open file. Improving one piece at a time often leads to positions where tactics appear naturally, because all the pieces are aimed at the same targets.

## Restricting enemy pieces
The other side of piece activity is limiting your opponent's pieces. You can take squares away with pawns, block a bishop's diagonal, trade off their best piece, or keep their rooks away from open files. A piece that has no good squares can sometimes be trapped. Restricting the opponent is especially effective when combined with prophylaxis: preventing the moves that would activate their passive pieces.

## How to spot it in your games
To check piece activity during a game, count the squares each of your pieces controls and compare them with your opponent's pieces. Ask:
- Which of my pieces is doing the least, and where would it be better?
- Are my rooks connected and on open or half-open files?
- Is my opponent's best piece worth trading off?
Common mistakes include leaving a piece undeveloped for the whole game, placing knights on the edge, and making pawn moves that lock in your own bishop. Whenever you feel stuck, improve your worst piece.
