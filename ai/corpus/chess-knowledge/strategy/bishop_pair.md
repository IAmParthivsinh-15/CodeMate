# Bishop Pair

## Definition
The bishop pair means having both bishops while your opponent has lost one or both of theirs, usually in exchange for a knight. Because one bishop controls only light squares and the other only dark squares, a single bishop covers half the board, but two bishops together cover every square and complement each other perfectly. In open positions the bishop pair is generally considered an advantage worth around half a pawn. It is one of the most reliable long-term advantages in chess, and strong players often accept small concessions to obtain it.

## When the bishop pair is strongest
The bishop pair shines in certain positions:
- Open positions with few central pawns, where long diagonals are clear.
- Positions with pawns on both sides of the board, where the bishops' long range matters most.
- Endgames, where two bishops can control a passed pawn's path from a distance and restrict enemy knights.
- Positions where the opponent's pawns are fixed on one colour, giving the bishop of the other colour many targets.
In closed positions with locked pawn chains, knights can be as good or better, because they jump over pawns and use outposts.

## How to play with the bishop pair
To make the most of the bishop pair:
- Open the position with pawn breaks, since open lines favour bishops.
- Avoid exchanging one of your bishops unless you gain something concrete; keeping both is the point.
- Use pawns to take away squares from enemy knights, reducing their outposts.
- In the endgame, push your pawns forward on the side where the bishops are strongest, using them to control squares in front of the pawns.

## How to play against the bishop pair
To fight the bishop pair, keep the position closed, set up a solid pawn structure with fixed pawns, and seek strong outposts for your knights. Exchanging one of the bishops, even for a knight, removes the pair's special strength. Once one bishop has been exchanged, it often helps to place your pawns on squares of the opposite colour to your opponent's remaining bishop, so it has fewer targets, but be careful not to create weak squares of the other colour.

## How to spot it in your games
When you consider trading a bishop for a knight, ask whether it gives your opponent the bishop pair and whether the position is likely to open. If yes, the trade may be a long-term concession. When you gain the bishop pair, look for pawn breaks that open the center. Common mistakes include giving up a bishop early without reason, and, when holding the pair, keeping the position closed so the bishops never show their power.
