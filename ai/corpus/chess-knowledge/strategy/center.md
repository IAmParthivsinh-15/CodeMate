# The Center

## Definition
The center is the group of squares in the middle of the board, above all e4, d4, e5 and d5. The wider ring around them (c3 to f3 up to c6 to f6) is often called the extended center. Controlling the center matters because pieces placed there reach more squares and can switch quickly from one side of the board to the other. A knight on e5 attacks eight squares, while a knight on a1 attacks only two. Central control also gives you space, making it harder for your opponent's pieces to find good squares.

## Ways to control the center
There are two main approaches to the center. The classical approach occupies it with pawns, for example `1.e4` or `1.d4`, and supports them with pieces such as knights on f3 and c3. The hypermodern approach, used in openings like the King's Indian Defence, first controls the center from a distance with pieces, often a fianchettoed bishop on g7 or b7, and only later challenges the opponent's central pawns with pawn breaks like `...e5` or `...c5`. Both approaches aim at the same goal: the central squares must not belong only to your opponent.

## Central pawn structures
The shape of the center guides your plans:
- Open center: central pawns have been exchanged. Pieces become very active, bishops and rooks gain power, and development and king safety are urgent.
- Closed center: pawns are locked, for example White pawns on d5 and e4 against Black pawns on d6 and e5. Play shifts to the wings, often with pawn storms, and knights gain value.
- Fluid or tension center: pawns face each other but are not locked, so each side must decide when to exchange or push.
A strong pawn center gives space, but it can become a target if it is overextended or lacks support.

## How to spot it in your games
To use the center well, ask at every stage:
- Who controls e4, d4, e5 and d5? Count the pieces and pawns hitting each square.
- Is the center open, closed or fluid? That tells you whether to play in the middle or on the wings.
- Can my opponent strike at my central pawns with a pawn break?
Common mistakes include moving edge pawns in the opening instead of developing towards the center, allowing the opponent to build an unchallenged pawn center, and launching a wing attack while the center is still open, which lets the opponent counterattack through the middle.

## Key takeaways
The center is the most valuable part of the board because pieces there are most active. Occupy it with pawns or control it with pieces, and challenge your opponent's center before it becomes too strong. The type of center (open, closed or fluid) decides where you should play: a secure center allows action on the wings, while an unstable center demands attention first.
